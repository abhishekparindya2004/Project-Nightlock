SPHEREx: The Mission to Map the Universe in 102 Infrared Colors

Idea of SPHEREx

SPHEREx was conceived to map the universe using infrared light.

Builder of SPHEREx

NASA JPL led SPHEREx development with Caltech and industry partners.

Launch of SPHEREx

SPHEREx launched aboard SpaceX Falcon 9 on March 11, 2025./h3>

Objective of SPHEREx

Map galaxies, investigate cosmic origins, and study interstellar ice molecules.

The complete SPHEREx story: from an idea to a new map of the universe

1. What exactly is SPHEREx?

SPHEREx stands for Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer. It is a NASA space observatory designed to study the universe by observing the sky in 102 different wavelengths of near-infrared light. The mission is managed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL), with science leadership based at Caltech.

A guide from the NightLock Observatory team

Unlike a conventional space telescope that concentrates on individual objects or small regions, SPHEREx repeatedly surveys the entire sky. Its measurements help researchers investigate three enormous questions: How did the universe begin to develop its large-scale structure? Scientists want to test ideas about cosmic inflation, an extremely rapid expansion thought to have occurred very early in the universe's history. How did galaxies form and evolve? By measuring light from hundreds of millions of galaxies, researchers can investigate how galaxies are distributed in three-dimensional space and how the universe has changed over time. Where did the ingredients for life come from? SPHEREx maps water ice, carbon dioxide, and other molecules in our galaxy that help scientists understand the chemistry of star- and planet-forming regions. NASA Science +1 The important thing to understand is that SPHEREx is not designed to detect extraterrestrial life directly. It studies the cosmic conditions and chemical ingredients relevant to the origins of planets and potentially life-supporting environments.

Not every close approach becomes a bright naked-eye spectacle. Visibility depends on the comet’s activity, distance, position in the sky, and the darkness of your observing site. Check a current astronomy forecast before heading out, and let your eyes adjust to the dark.

Choose a clear view and give your eyes time

Find a safe location away from bright streetlights, with an unobstructed horizon in the direction where the comet is expected. A pair of binoculars can help reveal a diffuse glow that is difficult to notice at first. Keep the view steady, scan slowly, and avoid using a phone screen at full brightness.

Look for a soft glow, not a sharp point

Through binoculars or a small telescope, a comet may look like a hazy patch rather than a crisp star. Its coma surrounds the nucleus, while dust and ion tails can point in different directions, shaped by sunlight and the solar wind. Sketching what you see or taking a short series of photographs can help you notice subtle changes over time.

“The best observing session is the one that leaves you curious enough to come back tomorrow.”

A thoughtful approach to every observing session

Comets move on their own schedules, so patience matters. Dress for the weather, bring a red-filtered light, and protect your night vision. Never look at the Sun through binoculars or a telescope; solar observing requires equipment designed and certified for that purpose.

Whether the comet appears as a faint smudge or a striking streak in a photograph, taking time to follow its changing position connects a moment in your backyard with the long history of our Solar System.
